Early deﬁ  nitions of 
gentriﬁ  cation referred to a process by which 
working-class residential neighbourhoods were rehabilitated by middle-class home-buyers, landlords and professional developers. In the process, a reinvestment of capital at the urban centre occurred and the original, typically working-class inhabits of an area, were replaced by a more afﬂ  uent class of people

mutated over time” (Lees et al. , 2008, p. 129)

Central-city Districts
Cape T own, established in 1652, is South 
Africa’s oldest Western city. The city is home to 3 million residents. Given its age, it is no stranger to change. In the 20th century, directed change included population re-movals, construction of dormitory Black and Coloured townships on the Cape Flats and suburbanisation of workplaces and resid-ences north and south of the old CBD along tram tracks, railway lines and motorways (Pirie, 2007). In the process, the historical ‘centre’ of Cape T own became increasingly off-centre, geographically speaking, located towards the north-west of the 70-km peninsula (see Figure 2) reduced to a parcel of land cradled by Table Mountain, occupy-ing less than 1 per cent of the metropolitan area and housing a negligible proportion

the 1950s and remains intense, spread out in an arc of development starting in the south-west neighbourhoods of Muizenburg, Claremont and Newlands, across to Century City, the Tygerberg, Brakenfell towards Somerset West in the south-east of the metropole. Some Other Consequences of the 
Cape Town Partnership
Pirie (2007) argues, as does McDonald (2008), 
Miraftab (2007) and Lemanski (2007), that as is the case in many cities elsewhere, private–public partnerships that have been installed as quasi-urban government are not always welcomed. In the Cape T own case, concern has been expressed over whether the CTP can serve the interests of informal entrepre-neurs and homeless people (see in particular, Miraftab, 2007). Indeed, Lemanski (2007) elevates these concerns to a broader terrain by questioning the wisdom of ‘Southern’ cities, such as Cape T own, seeking to become globally competitive through ﬂ  agship CBD 
regeneration (and subsequently gentriﬁ  ed), 
whilst also having to address domestic socio-economic redistribution to a desperately poor urban community outside Cape T own’s gentrifying central districts. In hindsight, commentators argue, these people are totally excluded from these areas both literally and figuratively. Moreover, anxiety has been expressed about the displacement of crime into surrounding neighbouring districts which has, in fact, led to the development of new ‘improvement districts’ (CCIDs) (see Miraftab, 2007). The prominence of estab-lished commercial interests in the CTP raises queries about the commodiﬁ  cation 
of heritage and property, and also about the narrowing of public policy debates and the ending of meaningful public participation in service delivery (Pirie, 2007). McDonald (2008) suggests that these outcomes should be understood as the result of ‘neo-liberal localisation’ . Some commentators read the emergence of the CTP as the result of a weak city government abrogating its civic respon-sibilities in the face of private pressure (Pirie, 2007). Others argue that the terms ‘security’ and ‘safety’ , today, rally support for neo-liberal planning just as the terms ‘sanitation’ and ‘hygience’ did for 19th-century urban reformism and colonial planning (Miraftab, 
2007). Whilst Cape T own has since the incep-tion of the CPT seen the rise of a stronger local government, the fact that the new incum-bents of local political power are decidedly more neo-liberal in political economic outlook, might further push back the role of local government, not as a consequence of poor urban management, but by design (McDonald, 2008). Moreover, for the recently arrived 55 000 ‘new middle class’ residents occupying the newly redeveloped CBD, this move towards greater privatisation will probably be embraced and encouraged. Indeed, McDonald’s (2008) identiﬁ  cation of 
neo-liberal localisation (see Table 1) appears very accurate.

torically, there is inevitably concern about centrally sited residential developments from which the mass of daily downtown workers and shoppers are excluded by virtue of their poverty. New Prospects for South African 
Gentriﬁ  cation Studies
Since 2000, there has been a signiﬁ  cant shift 
in the cycle of decline witnessed in South Africa’s inner-city areas. The introduction of city-centre improvement districts (CCIDs) and major infrastructure investment, includ-ing transport and conference facilities, appear to be reversing the trend of decay. These pro-cesses of change do not always take place in an even manner, both between and in cities, and central Cape T own has probably made the most progress, certainly as a proportion of the physical urban landscape (Miraftab, 2007). As highlighted, a range of multibillion rand redevelopment projects, such as the VandA Waterfront area, the Foreshore and the CBD have been transformed into new spaces for the new middle classes for living, working and leisure, and have gripped the public imagination across the country—gentriﬁ  cation in both its narrow and more 
expansive meaning is now ﬁ  rmly established. 
Moreover, as demonstrated, there are clear links between state investment in the CBD, linked to desires for state-led global competi-tiveness and subsequent gentriﬁ  cation.
This nexus of concerns is also increasingly 
evident elsewhere in South Africa. The much publicised ‘Blue IQ’ projects in Gauteng—South Africa’s most global city-region—are probably the most far-reaching in their in-tention of regenerating large parts of inner-city Johannesburg (Blue IQ, 2002). What is important to note is that these developments 
are heavily impacted by local, regional and national government frameworks and agencies’ increased desire for a better position in the ‘global city stakes’ . The local state, though never stating so explicitly, is aiming to provide the catalysts of urban regener-ation after which the private sector, it would appear, then follows. This claim holds equal relevance for the major CBD and waterfront redevelopment seen in cities such as Pretoria and Durban respectively. It is also in the con-text of properties ‘being ripe for redevelop-ment’ and the introduction of a range of facilities, often drawn upon by the ‘new middle class’ , that possibilities arise for gentriﬁ  cation 
in its more expansive interpretation. What needs to be appreciated is that current third-wave, and potentially fourth-wave, debates concerning gentriﬁ  cation are of direct rele-
vance to the ‘Southern’ urban context. Against this backdrop, then, we may interpret what follows as providing some ideas for future gentrification research in South African urban areas.
First, the fundamental point underlying 
the gentriﬁ  cation literature, whether local 
or international, is that the process implies displacement and exclusion of a lower- by a higher-class group. This holds potential for engagement with South African cities both historically and in the present. Although volumes of research have been completed on apartheid  displacement (Meth, 2000), there has

interest relates to rural gentriﬁ  cation in South 
Africa. Greater, state-sponsored integration into the global agricultural economy and increased competition, not least of which are massive farm subsidies in developed Northern countries, have resulted in the creation of a post-productivist country-side (see Müller, 2006, 2007) in many parts of South Africa. The idea of ‘saving’ rural towns and communities is hardly new in South Africa, but within a context where the national spatial development framework has effect-ively relegated large numbers of towns to ‘basket cases’ owing to the fact that they are unlikely to compete effectively in the global economic arena, has resulted in minimal national and provincial government invest-ment in those places. They are now simply left to fend for themselves. Rural towns once servicing local farming communities are now forced to look towards other economic sectors as farming units consolidate, become ever larger with fewer workers, or are simply not viable. Conclusion
New-build and ofﬁ  ce conversion have been 
central to the quick and dramatic redevel-opment of Cape T own’s CBD. Whilst gen-triﬁ cation is certainly not a stranger to the 
neighbouring areas of central Cape T own, its CBD was in serious decline until very recently. What has emerged from our review is that the impact of national and local gov-ernment development frameworks played a key role in the redevelopment of central Cape T own. Once the state decided to facilitate the regeneration of the CBD, the market, in its many guises, immediately ‘came to the rescue’ . This ‘rescue plan’ is, however, linked to very speciﬁ  c objectives—gaining a foothold in the global cities’ stakes. We suspect that 
these ‘global’ ambitions will only strengthen in time. The narrative presented here does not demonstrate displacement central to the classic understanding of gentriﬁ  cation. How-
ever, the CBD’s target market is the very same people associated with gentriﬁ  cation 
and those displaced by default, no different from those usually affected by this process. Consequently, this CBD narrative is not about who is there , but rather who is not . As 
indicated in the ﬁ  nal section, these concerns 
do not only hold potential relevance to central-city areas, but indeed hold currency (and arguably more signiﬁ  cantly) elsewhere 
in the South African urban hierarchy. What is perhaps most important to note is that a wide range of gentrification debates, not least of which are the impact and issues sur-rounding new-build gentrification, are of direct relevance to the South African context and, we would argue, will become of ever-greater significance in the future. We suspect this is probably the case in many other ‘Southern’ cities too. In this respect, Jennifer Robinson’s (2005) call for seeking out greater comparative research has par-ticular resonance.

Tongji UniversityAbstract
Accompanying the functional transformation of rural areas from productivism
to postproductivism, the rural areas wi th scenic views surrounding megacities
are re ‐evaluated in terms of their importa nce as destinations for visitors,
especially in the case of booming bed and breakfast (B&B) industry in con-
temporary rural China. While private in vestment has generally led to improve-
ments in the physical space and economic development of these villages, there
is also concern about the negative effects of rural gentrification, such as thecommercialisation of rural space, the alienation of rural culture and the dis-
placement of indigenous residents. Thi s study examines the multifaceted nature
of rural gentrification by observing two villages within the Moganshan
Mountain area —the birthplace of rural B&Bs in China, and explores how these
processes have been leveraged to promote rural revitalisation. Research find-ings suggest that disparities in original r esources and property rights allocation
underpinning the B&B industries among the first ‐wave gentrifiers and the local
villagers, when viewed through the lens of linked migration, may lead to
different socioeconomic outcomes. The B&B industry's growth can either be
exclusive, primarily favouring gentrifi ers, or complementar y, fostering mutual
benefits and learning opportunities for both gentrifiers and local villagers.
Moreover, forming a collaborative networ k of rural villagers, returning migrants,
and external elites can contribute to rural revitalisation through gentrification.
These findings contribute to the theoreti cal understanding of rural gentrifica-
tion by adding evidence from the Globa l South and provide insights to inform
Chinese rural revitalisation policies, especially alternatives to traditional
state ‐led revitalisation projects.

1|INTRODUCTION
Urban ‐rural inequality has increased substantially as a result of
China's rapid urbanisation (Long & Liu, 2016 ). Consequently,
China's rural areas are often associated with poverty, abandon-ment and depopulation, in contrast to the beautiful rural idyll
commonly depicted in Anglophone countries, such as the UK
(Phillips, 1993 ,2005 ). In response to rural decline, the Chinese
central government has explored different methods to revitaliseits vast rural areas and improve the well ‐being of rural residents,
such as the ‘new socialist villages ’initiative (CPC Central Com-
mittee, & State Council, 2006 ) or more recently the ‘beautiful
village construction ’(Ministry of Agriculture, 2013 )a n d ‘rural re-
vitalisation strategy ’(CPC Central Committee & State Council,
2018 ). However, these progressive policies —especially the earlier
ones —were mainly implemented through state ‐led financial
assistance and support. The effectiveness and sustainability of
these projects have often been limited due to the lack of bottom ‐
up initiatives (Shen & Shen, 2019 ). Given this situation, China's
Strategic Plan of Rural Revitalisation (2018 –2022) recognises that
private investment should be lev eraged to support rural devel-
opment (CPC Central Committee & State Council, 2018 ). In other
words, the state might focus more on institutional design and
favourable policymaking to attract private investors to rural areas
and encourage bottom ‐up initiatives.
While much of rural China is associated with depopulation and
economic decline, recent years have seen the emergence of rural
gentrification in areas known for their outstanding natural en-vironment and slow pace of life —for example, the famous tourist
destinations of Dali and Lijiang in Yunnan province (Sun &
Xu,2017 ;Z h a o , 2019 ). In orthodox ‘rural gentrification ’literature,
Neil Smith reminds us that the ‘rent gap ’between the devaluation
of properties within an area and the anticipated financial returns
is important to understand the motivation of gentrification
and relevant capital investme nts into the built environment
(Smith, 1979a ,1979b ,2002a ). The rent gap highlights the
opportunity to reap greater rental revenues through investment.
Phillips ( 2005 , p.479) suggests rural gentrification may be seen as
‘one form of the revalorisation of resources and spaces which
have become seen as unproductive or marginal to agrarian capital ’.
With rising de ‐agriculturalization and the transition to post ‐
productivism (Burton & Wilson, 2006 ), the value of rural resources
and spaces has been increasingly recognised. The attractiveness
of rural areas is increased by their ‘proximity to wilderness ame-
nities ’(Darling, 2005 , p. 1023; Snepenger et al., 1995 ).
The rent gap and the resulting rental inflation in gentrified
communities cause displacement of the lower socioeconomic strata
(Ding et al., 2015 ; Freeman, 2005 ; Ghose, 2004 ). In most cases,
displaced residents who cannot afford the higher rents move to
neighbourhoods with lower rents (Ding et al., 2015 ). Consequently,
gentrification is often regarded as a somewhat conflicted term.Nevertheless, under China's unique rural land ownership structure,
whereby a house and rural land cannot be sold by individuals, localvillagers are not typical victims displaced in the gentrification pro-
cess (Qian et al., 2013 ;Z h a o , 2019 ). Instead, the presence of rural
gentrifiers creates opportunities for local villagers to charge rent
and become active players in promoting rural socioeconomic re-
structuring. In this case, the ongo ing rural gentrification in some
villages might positively impact local villages without much state
intervention.
Our case study in villages of the Moganshan Mountain area, in
Zhejiang Province reveals the multifaceted essence of rural gentrifi-cation and the possibility of positive impacts on rural development.
Natural resources in the Moganshan Mountain area and its proximity
to the neighbouring megacities of Shanghai, Hangzhou and Nanjinghave made a prosperous local tourism industry possible, creating the
boom of B&B (bed and breakfast) businesses in the area. This trend
started in 2007 when gentrifiers began to invest in high ‐end guest
houses in the local villages of Moganshan. Villagers' homes were
increasingly redeveloped, transformed, and refurbished to accom-
modate tourists. Unintentionally, the ongoing gentrification inMoganshan somewhat satisfies the government's anticipation that
rural revitalisation would increase farmers' income, creating a thriving
rural industry and encouraging return migration.
In light of the potentially positive impacts, to what extent can
gentrification contribute to rural revitalisation? Drawing on the
concept of linked migration, our research questions are: (1) How has
the local B&B industry thrived and influenced the socioeconomictransformation of rural communities? (2) How has rural gentrification
encouraged the return migration of local villagers and other urban ‐to‐
rural migration? Based on two villages in Moganshan, this researchadds information on the Global South to the literature to enhance the
contemporary understanding of gentrification.
The remainder of this paper comprises four sections. Section 2
reviews the literature on gentrification, rural gentrification, andlinked migration and proposes a conceptual framework. Section 3
introduces the case study area and research methods. Section 4
outlines the research findings, and Section 5discusses the findings
and draws conclusions.
2|RURAL GENTRIFICATION AND LINKED
MIGRATION: A CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK
2.1 |Gentrification and rural gentrification
Many scholars believe the definition and outcomes of gentrification
hinge on an influx of capital, social displacement, and related social,economic, physical, and cultural transformations (Atkinson, 2003 ;
Brown ‐Saracino, 2010 ). The literature regards middle ‐class coloni-
sation, and the resulting displacement of indigenous residents, as adefining characteristic or pre ‐requisite for specific stages of gentri-
fication (Glass, 1964 ). However, this argument is increasingly con-
tested (Butler, 2007 ; Stockdale, 2010 ). Phillips ( 2002 ), for example,
acknowledges that the inflow of the middle ‐class population may not
necessarily occur with social displacement. Moreover, it is asserted2o f1 6 |CHEN ET AL.
that ‘the diversity of cases challenges any single definition of
or explanation for gentrification ’(Brown ‐Saracino, 2010 , p.355).
Hackworth ( 2002 , p. 815) defines gentrification as ‘the production of
space for progressively more affluent users ’. Additionally, ‘the habi-
tation of vacant or derelict property ’(Atkinson, 2003 , p. 2344) or
inner ‐city revitalisation (Ley, 1986 ) can also be included under the
umbrella definition of gentrification.
Notwithstanding debates on its definition and resultant out-
comes, research on gentrification has proliferated since the termwas coined in the 1960s (Atkinson & Bridge, 2004 ). While it spreads
from the Global North to the Global South, it also cascades down
the hierarchies of urban systems within the Global North (e.g.,regional cities). Notably, the process of gentrification also extends
into rural settings. Here, the distinction between ‘urban ’and ‘rural ’
gentrification becomes blurred. Rural gentrification is a usefuldescription for contemporary rural social change, similar to change
in urban settings (Phillips, 1993 ). It can be defined as ‘the revalor-
isation of spaces formerly used by agrarian and other rural capitalsfor residential development [ …] retail, leisure and even industrial
uses ’(Phillips, 2004 ,p .1 4 ) .
Due to mounting housing costs, lower ‐income residents move
away from their original locations to areas with more affordableaccommodation —a process described as ‘the class ‐based colonisa-
tion of cheaper residential neighbourhoods ’(Atkinson, 2003 ,
p. 2343). After gentrification, a series of economic, demographic,and aesthetic transformations happen over the following decade
(Brown ‐Saracino, 2010 ). There is long ‐term debate on whether
gentrification works as a harbinger of renaissance or if it has adestructive impact on the working class through urban restructuring
(Brown ‐Saracino, 2010 ). For example, a systematic literature review
of English ‐language studies confirmed that gentrification had largely
negative impacts, such as social displacement, segregation andpolarisation (Atkinson, 2002 ).
In the European context, the restructuring process of gentrifi-
cation is not often portrayed as negative (Atkinson, 2003 ). The extent
and nature of harm associated with it remain debatable. While sys-
temic social inequalities and the negative impacts on long ‐
term re-
sidents cannot be ignored (Brown ‐Saracino, 2010 ), proponents fa-
vouring market solutions, particularly on the political right, argue that
the social costs are negligible, especially when weighed against a
backdrop of decaying neighbourhoods. Consequently, they prefersemantically neutral terms like ‘neighbourhood revitalisation ’or
‘renaissance ’(Lees, 2008 ). It is also suggested that recent gentrifi-
cation practices are less socially detrimental than earlier studiessuggested (Freeman & Braconi, 2004 ).
The unique aspects of rural gentrification in China are deeply
rooted in the significant legal, economic, and cultural disparities
between urban and rural areas (Yang & Loopmans, 2023 ). Because it
is difficult to sell rural land and houses in China, local villagers who
hold monopolised property rights are not typically displaced through
rural gentrification. Although the process of gentrification has gen-erated controversy over issues such as excessive commodification of
rural spaces, alienation of rural culture, the decommunisation of ruralsocieties, and conflicts of interest between local villagers and in ‐
migrants in Chinese rural areas, the positive impacts of rural gentri-fication should not be underestimated. Notably, the rapid renovation
of the village built environment, upgrading of industrial structures,
and return migration, have brought economic opportunities to localcommunities, mitigated the phenomenon of ‘hollowed ‐out’villages,
and contributed to the revitalisation of rural China (Yang & Xu, 2022 ).
2.2 |Linked migration
The theory of gentrification draws on both production ‐side and
consumption ‐side explanations. Smith ( 1979a ,1979b ,2002a )
suggests that the rent gap and resultant capital investments bring
about gentrification. Alternat ively, the consumption approach
asserts that population movement causes economic restructuring,
and a predilection for urban/rural amenities drives investment in
the built environment, accele rating gentrification (Ley, 1986 ).
However, Stockdale ( 2010 , p. 39) argues that gentrification itself
is‘a multifaceted process evolving through different stages and
giving rise to different outcomes ’, and therefore, both production ‐
side and consumption ‐side approaches are important.
Population movement brought about by gentrification is a
dynamic and complex social recomposition process, and this is
important for understanding the impact of rural gentrification(Phillips, 2010 ; Smith et al., 2021 ). Zhu et al. ( 2019 ) categorised these
interrelationships into three types —in‐migrants without original
connections, in ‐migrants based on social network connections and in ‐
migrants based on blood connections, which highlights the multi-
faceted aspect of rural gentrification.
Rural gentrification often stems from changes to the local labour
market triggered by new economic activities, and typically beginswith in ‐migrants who have no original connection to the destination
area. As argued by Sassen ( 2006 , p. 86), gentrification ‘ultimately
rests on the availability of a vast supply of low ‐wage workers ’, em-
phasising the existence of linked migration through complementary
labour market roles. In rural America, for example, the baby boomer
generation gentrifiers attracted a large Latino population who pro-vided them with services like construction and healthcare (Nelson
et al., 2010 ). However, the literature on linked migration extends
beyond labour market connections. For example, Zhu et al. ( 2019 )
found inter ‐regional migrants and intra ‐regional migrants are linked
through the rental housing market in peri ‐urban Shanghai.
Another example is ‘chain migration ’, which refers to the
migration of individuals aided by their family and social networks(such as relatives and fellow villagers). In contrast to in ‐migration
undertaken by independent individual migrants, chain migration
allows in ‐migrants to obtain informat ion on local labour market
opportunities from their interconnected social networks. Subse-
quent generations of migrants are influenced by the favourable
information provided by the first ‐generation migrants, and a
‘multiplier effect ’is created in the local area (Helmenstein &
Yegorov, 2000 ).CHEN ET AL. |3o f1 6
The third type of linked migration is driven by family strategies,
where individuals move to maximise household interests by diversi-fying livelihood strategies. In China, the enduring impact of the
urban ‐rural dual system has resulted in a pattern of migration based
on blood ties characteristic of urban ‐rural cyclicality (Chen &
Fan, 2018 ). While family separation is often viewed as a consequence
of migration (Ryan, 2011 ), migration is frequently employed as a
household strategy to enhance the overall well ‐being of families in
developing countries (Zhu et al., 2022 ). Outbound migrants take
advantage of favourable conditions in both rural and urban settings
to strengthen the family's ability to withstand risks and seize
opportunities, ultimately aiming to break this cycle of separation (Fanet al., 2011 ; Xu et al., 2022).
In this case study, the gentrification of Moganshan Township was
initially driven by rural B&Bs run by first ‐generation gentrifiers. This
development subsequently facilitated chain migration and family
migration, and a multifaceted social network was established that
spurred economic, social, and cultural transformation of the ruralcommunity.
Phillips ( 2005 ), suggested that the flow of capital associated with
gentrification does not only target residential projects but alsoassociated retail and leisure facilities. This reveals the dynamics andcomplexity of gentrification, which ‘brings changes in leisure and
retail activities, consumption patterns [to cater to the tastes ofgentrifiers] ’in addition to the flow of capital and population move-
ment (Solana ‐Solana, 2010 , p. 508; Zukin et al., 2009 ). Linked
migration associated with gentrification is demonstrated by the flows
between urban and rural areas (Douglass, 1998 ). Newly arrived re-
sidents from rural areas might rely on people and services in urbanareas (Nelson et al., 2010 ). In this context, it is particularly important
to understand the migration pattern of rural gentrification and its
socioeconomic impacts triggered by the development of rural B&Bsin China.
2.3 |Analytical framework
Hamnett ( 1991 ) pointed out that gentrification is a complex ‘physical,
economic, social, and cultural phenomenon ’. Economic restructuring
impacts resource ‐based industries, leading to the supply of gentrifi-
able properties (Nelson et al., 2014 ), and forming new class structures
at destinations to achieve social reorganisation. When these inves-tors, new consumers, and labour forces arrive in rural communities,
they fundamentally alter the local economic, social and physical
landscape (Nelson et al., 2010 ). The emerging connections between
in‐migrants and local villagers extend beyond mere employment
associations within the workforce, and develop into novel social in-
teractions. Our study goes beyond the production/consumption

4.1 |In‐migrants without original ties: The arrival
of the first ‐generation gentrifiers
4.1.1 |Laoling Village: The arrival of the
international investors
Before the emergence of the rural B&B industry, Laoling Village was a
traditional rural mountain community where local inhabitants workedin agriculture and rudimentary product processing. In 2007, there
was a dramatic increase in nutrient loading to Taihu Lake, not far
from Moganshan Township, resulting in the notorious ‘cyanobacterial
bloom event ’that drew attention from academia and higher ‐level
governments. In response, a series of policies and regulations were
promulgated to control industrial wastewater discharge into theTaihuLake basin, and thousands of polluting enterprises were rectified orclosed. Most of them were small factories or cottage industries in
surrounding counties and towns, characterised by small scale, out-
moded technology, obsolete equipment, poor management, andheavy consumption of water resources. Their closure significantly
reduced the local villagers' household income. Like others in this
region, working ‐age locals in Laoling Village had to migrate to cities in
search of employment opportunities, exacerbating the ageing and
hollowing out of the local rural communities.
However, in 2007, there was a turning point in the ongoing
decline of Laoling Village, when a South African entrepreneur iden-
tified a potential investment opportunity during a holiday in the
Moganshan Mountain area. He turned seven vacant farmhouses inLaoling Village into the first high ‐end rural B&Bs in Moganshan
Township, known as the ‘Naked Stables ’. Subsequently, international
investors from France and South Korea, and domestic investors from
places like Shanghai and Hangzhou followed and extended this trend,leading to a rapid increase in the number of rural B&Bs in Moganshan
Town. The region became an iconic destination for rural weekend
vacations in the Yangtze River Delta region. According to the statis-tics on tourist consumption, visitors' expenditure on accommodation
reached 850 Chinese Yuan (CNY) and accounted for 64.3% of the percapita tourist expenditure in Laoling Village, much higher figures than
the average levels in Zhejiang Province and Huzhou City (Table 2).
The arrival of international investors resulted in the rapid
development of the local B&B indu stry, which yielded substantial
economic returns for investors, and increased tax revenues for thelocal government. In 2018, the tourism industry in Moganshan
Township contributed 85 million CNY in taxes, with over 60 mil-
lion CNY of that amount attributable to the high ‐end B&B desti-
nations, such as ‘Naked Home Village ’and ‘Naked Castle ’.T h i s
demonstrates the pivotal role of high ‐end B&Bs in the develop-
ment of the B&B industry in Laoling Village, as stated by the
mayor of Moganshan Township:
International investments have played a significant role
in the development of Laoling Village, with elite outsid-ers playing a leading role. The success of international
investors' projects can be attributed in part to the
inclusiveness of the local community. The concept ofhigh ‐end B&Bs can facilitate government initiatives and
create a win ‐win situation for non ‐local investors, local
villagers, and the local government. (Interview with the
town's mayor (2017 ‐8), No.7)

4.1.2 |Xiantan village: Diversified first ‐generation
gentrifiers
Similar to Laoling Village, a significant proportion of the local villagers
of Xiantan Village also out ‐migrated in response to the factory clo-
sures brought about by regional environmental regulation enforce-
ment in the Taihu Lake basin. However, what distinguishes them isthat before the rise of the rural B&B industry, Xiantan Village hadalready witnessed the emergence of traditional farm stays ( nong jia
le). Since 2013, some domestic white ‐collar workers from Shanghai,
Hangzhou, and other cities recognised Xiantan Village's potential as arecreational destination because of its idyllic rural scenery. Their
investment in rural B&Bs was often smaller in scale than in Laoling
Village (ranging from 3 to 5 million CNY per project). However, thephysical landscape and services were similar. As a result, local vil-
lagers were inspired to open B&Bs, and a group of out ‐migrants and
entrepreneurs were encouraged to return to their hometowns tostart long ‐term B&B ventures, rather than leasing their homesteads
to nonlocal B&B investors for short ‐term returns. As one of the
villager cadres of Xiantan Village stated:
The B&Bs in Xiantan Village are mainly run by young
people returning home and middle ‐aged people with

5|DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Compared to the nuanced scholarly debate on rural gentrification
in Europe and the US (Nelson et al., 2010 ; Nelson et al.,
2015 ;P h i l l i p s , 2002 ;S m i t h , 2002a ), there has been little research
on the concept in Global South countries, including China(Phillips et al., 2023 ). However, our case study findings on the
villages with booming B&B industries in Moganshan resonate withextant research on various aspects. First, the gentrified rural areas
in our study feature the replacem ent of traditional agricultural
production with amenities and cultural landscapes. This is similart ot h ed e v e l o p m e n to ft h ec o m m u t e rb e l ti nt h eU Kc o u n t r y s i d e
(Phillips et al., 2023 ). Second, rural gentrification is driven by
urban ‐rural migration, or counter ‐urbanisation motivated by the
promise of a rural idyll (Liu et al., 2023 ; Phillips, 1993 ,2005 ). This
provides opportunities to invest t he surplus capital accumulated in
contemporary global cities and reg ional urban centres, seeking out
new spaces in potentially profitable rent gaps, much like whathappened in the UK and US (Nelson & Hines, 2018 ). Third, our
findings are consistent with extant research that suggests eco-
nomic and sociocultural changes are reshaping rural areas intodynamic spaces of migration and population churn (Nelson &
Hines, 2018 ; Smith et al., 2021 ;T a n g&Z h u , 2020 ), which may also
lead to various forms of displacement (Marcuse, 1985 ; Phillips
et al., 2020 ; Phillips et al., 2021 ; Smith et al., 2019 ).
Although we argue that the process of rural gentrification could
be leveraged to promote rural revitalisation in the Chinese context,we acknowledge that even with the same physical landscape, the
economic, sociocultural transformations, and sustainability challenges
that underpin this process may differ considerably. In recognising themultifaceted nature of rural gentrification, this research goes beyondthe production/consumption debate and examines the interplay
between the economic, social and physical transformation and the
changes in population composition due to linked migration in twovillages in the Moganshan Mountain area. It examines to what extent
gentrification can facilitate rural revitalisation, particularly in countr-
ies with a tradition of smallholder farming. These findings provideinsights into the Chinese rural policies, offering alternatives to tra-
ditional state ‐led projects for revitalising rural areas, and shed light on

how to improve the rural development in countries and across the
Global South.
First, by observing the rural gentrification process in two
villages within the Moganshan Mountain area through the lens of
linked migration, it becomes evident that the disparities in originalresources and property rights allocation between the first ‐
generation gentrifiers and the indigenous villagers underpinning
the B&B industry may lead to different socioeconomic outcomes,necessitating systematic policy interventions. It has been sug-gested that indigenous villagers in China, acting as landlords, may
eventually benefit from rural gentrification, potentially exerting
pressure on nonlocal investors if l and rent or property prices rise.
This highlights the fundamental role of institutional ‐structural
settings in the process of rural gentrification (Yang et al., 2018 ).
However, most of the first ‐generation nonlocal investors took
long ‐term leases of 15 −20 years on vacant homesteads, creating a
segregated high ‐end B&B market. This compelled local villagers to
operate lower ‐tier B&Bs or pursue alternative work opportunities,
thus suffering displacement in terms of development opportuni-
ties. The first generation of non local investors in Xiantan only
worked as harbingers of B&B industries with small ‐scale invest-
ment in a small proportion of rural homesteads. This inspired localvillagers, return migrants and late ‐coming entrepreneurs ’ambi-
tions to open B&Bs.
Second, the disparity in first ‐generation gentrifiers has led to
different patterns of rural economic restructuring and linked migra-
tion; therefore, different rural development outcomes. In Laoling
Village, the process can be recognised as so ‐called ‘planetary urba-
nisation ’(Phillips et al., 2022 ), where the urban ‐to‐rural migration
increases the rural population and spreads urban lifestyles (Cook and
Phillips, 2005 ). The first ‐generation gentrifiers created a profitable
business model of exclusive B&Bs for the urban middle ‐class and
then attracted nonlocal college graduates as the second ‐generation
gentrifiers, dominating the upper ‐level job market and leaving the
local villagers with only lower ‐level job opportunities. In contrast, the
process of gentrification in Xiantan Village was that the first ‐
generation gentrifiers included both nonlocal investors and returning
locals (Chan et al., 2016 ). This group not only cared about the sym-
bolic and affective senses of rurality as proposed by Phillips et al.
(2022 ), but also about facilitating the integration of local villagers
(especially their relatives with blood ties) into the B&B businesses.These returning local villagers were able to facilitate collaboration
between the nonlocal B&B investors and the not ‐yet‐gentrified vil-
lagers. Therefore, once policy and institutional settings attract theright first ‐generation gentrifiers, a more collaborative development
model involving local villagers, returning residents, and nonlocal
investors attracted through linked migration will effectively contrib-
ute to rural revitalisation through gentrification.
Finally, as illustrated by our case study villages in the Moganshan
Scenic Area, the process of rural gentrification in the Global South is
usually contextualised in a less developed economy with a starkurban ‐rural divide and significant rural out ‐migration, resulting in abrain drain and hollowed ‐out villages —conditions largely absent in
the UK and North America. An influx of surplus capital into ruralareas has led to the re ‐employment of the labour force left behind in
rural areas, increased income for local villagers catering to tourists,
and improved environmental amenities. These changes bring positiveexternalities to the non ‐gentrified households in the countryside,
even at the cost of some local villagers suffering displacement (which
only happens when the leases of rural homesteads are valid).
Moreover, rural areas are expected to assume more diversified
functions in the post ‐productivist era. However, this transformation
cannot be achieved solely by the local villagers as they often fail to
recognise the significant rent gap between actual returns from agri-cultural production and potential returns from converting land to
recreational amenities, cultural landscapes, or ecosystem services.
Consequently, urban ‐to‐rural migration could facilitate social learning
between gentrifiers, local villagers, and visitors, thereby altering the
conditions of entrepreneurial supply in areas where opportunities are
closely linked (Chan et al., 2016 ; Kalantaridis, 2010 , p. 1274),
strengthening the endogenous forces for rural revitalisation.
